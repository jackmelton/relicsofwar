Identification Guide

Civil War Cartridge Boxes

Leather survives the war less often than brass and iron — which makes an honest cartridge box a prize, and a well-aged reproduction a trap. Here's how to tell them apart.

Quick Identification

What it is
A leather pouch that held paper cartridges in two internal tins.
Worn
On a waist belt or over the shoulder on a wide leather sling.
Federal plates
Oval "US" box plate on the outer flap; round eagle plate on the sling.
Inside
Two removable tins with compartments for cartridges.
Markings
Arsenal or maker stamps may appear on the flap or inner surfaces.

Know the Object

The cartridge box is the leather pouch that carried a soldier's ammunition — paper cartridges stacked in two tin liners inside the box. Federal boxes for the .58 caliber rifle-musket are the most common surviving type. Many carry an oval "US" box plate on the outer flap and, when worn on the shoulder, a round eagle plate on the sling. A smaller companion, the cap box, held percussion caps on the belt.

Confederate and militia boxes exist in more variation and are scarcer; plain and captured-Federal boxes were widely used in the South.

Read the Leather and the Fittings

Original boxes show period leather — hand-finished, hand- or period-machine stitched, dried and aged in a way that's hard to fake convincingly. Check the pattern and dimensions against documented types, look for the tins inside (often missing, which lowers value but is common), and examine any plates for the same authenticity tests used on belt plates. Arsenal or maker markings on the flap add confidence when correct.

Real vs. Reproduction — The Tells

Reproduction cartridge boxes are made in quantity for reenactors and are sometimes aged and sold as originals. These are the tells.

1
Modern leather & stitching. Too-supple leather, bright edges, and even machine stitching point to a recent box.
2
Reproduction plates. The US and eagle plates on a faked box are often themselves modern castings — apply the belt-plate tests.
3
Fake aging. Uniform darkening, applied "grime," or scuffing that looks staged rather than earned.
4
Wrong pattern. Dimensions or flap shape that match no documented box, or "Confederate" boxes with fantasy features.
5
Fresh markings. Crisp, modern-font arsenal stamps on otherwise "aged" leather.

For any marked or plated box, send us the flap, plates, interior, and any stamps.

What Drives the Value

  • Completeness — original tins, both plates, and an intact sling raise value sharply.
  • Markings — clear, correct arsenal or maker stamps.
  • Condition — sound leather, unbroken stitching, no major repairs.
  • Rarity and attribution — Confederate, marked, or documented boxes command more.

Frequently Asked Questions

My box is missing the tins — does that ruin it?

No. Tins are often missing and can sometimes be replaced with correct originals. A complete box is worth more, but a sound box without tins is still collectible.

Should I oil the dry leather?

Be very cautious. Aggressive oils and modern conditioners can darken and damage antique leather and reduce value. Consult a specialist before treating a genuine box.

Is the plate on my box real?

Judge it exactly as you would a loose belt plate — construction, hooks, and patina. See our belt plate guide.
