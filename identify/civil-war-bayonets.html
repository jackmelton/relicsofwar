Identification Guide

Civil War Bayonets

Socket or sword? Federal or import? The pattern, the fit, and the markings will tell you — if you know where to look before you trust a seller's story.

Quick Identification

Two families
Socket bayonets (triangular blade, hollow socket) and sword/saber bayonets (with a grip and guard).
Socket type
Slides over the muzzle; used on rifle-muskets like the .58 Springfield and .577 Enfield.
Sword type
Locks onto a bayonet lug; used on many rifles and imported arms.
Markings
Inspector initials, maker names, and pattern marks appear on the socket, blade ricasso, or grip.
Fit
Genuine bayonets match the bore and lug of the arm they were made for.

Socket or Sword?

Start with the basic family. A socket bayonet has a triangular (three-fluted) blade and a hollow socket that slips over the muzzle of the rifle-musket, locking on a stud. This is the classic Civil War bayonet, carried by the great mass of Federal and Confederate infantry armed with weapons like the .58 Springfield and the imported .577 Enfield.

A sword (or saber) bayonet has a proper handle, a cross guard, and a longer edged blade; it attaches to a lug on the barrel. These were issued with arms fitted for them, including many two-band rifles and a range of imported patterns. The type of bayonet immediately narrows the family of arms it belongs to.

Read the Markings and the Fit

Original bayonets carry inspector and maker markings in the right places — small stamped initials, maker names, or arsenal marks on the socket, on the blade ricasso, or on the grip of a sword bayonet. The style and placement should match the documented pattern.

Equally important is fit and dimension. A genuine bayonet was made to a specification: socket diameter, blade length, and lug slot all correspond to a particular arm. A bayonet whose measurements don't match any known pattern — or that won't correctly index on the arm it's claimed for — is suspect regardless of its markings.

Real vs. Reproduction — The Tells

Reproduction bayonets are made for reenactors and, unfortunately, are also aged and sold as originals. These tells separate most copies from the real thing.

1
Wrong proportions. Blade length, socket diameter, or flute shape that doesn't match a documented pattern is the clearest giveaway.
2
Fake or wrong markings. Stamps in the wrong place, in modern fonts, or missing where they should be. Some copies have no markings at all.
3
Modern steel & finish. Bright, evenly "aged," or blued surfaces and crisp machine edges suggest recent manufacture.
4
Artificial pitting. Uniform, shallow, all-over "rust" that looks stippled or acid-etched rather than the irregular corrosion of a genuinely old blade.
5
Won't fit the arm. A bayonet that doesn't index and lock correctly on the weapon it's attributed to is a mismatch or a fake.

Before buying a marked or "identified" bayonet, send us the markings and full measurements.

What Drives the Value

  • Pattern and scarcity — some sword bayonets and Confederate-associated pieces are far scarcer than common Federal sockets.
  • Markings — clear, correct inspector and maker marks add confidence and value.
  • Condition — original surface, sharp flutes, working locking ring, and no repairs.
  • Matching scabbard — a correct, original scabbard adds meaningfully to a set.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a triangular bayonet always Civil War?

No. Triangular socket bayonets were used for a long span on both sides of the war and after. Pattern, dimensions, and markings — not the triangular shape alone — establish the period and origin.

My bayonet fits my Springfield — does that prove it's original?

Correct fit is a good sign, but reproductions are also made to fit. Fit plus correct dimensions, markings, and honest surface together make the case.

Should I sharpen or polish the blade?

Never. Polishing or sharpening destroys original surface and markings and sharply reduces collector value.
